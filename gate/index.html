A guard sits between a language model and the changes it proposes. It refuses what it cannot positively recognise, and it names the reason. Write your own proposal, or take one off the bench and try to break it. No model is called and no account is needed.

What the guard promises

A finding is a broken promise. These are the four it makes.

  1. It refuses what it cannot recognise. An action it does not know is denied, never waved through.
  2. It refuses anything that declares itself destructive, however that declaration is spelled. Not just the boolean true.
  3. It refuses a proposal missing a field its action requires, and a write that does not name the app it intends to touch.
  4. It refuses an oversized payload. Sixteen kilobytes is the limit.

      What counts as a finding

      • A false ALLOW is a finding. You broke one of the four promises above and the guard said ALLOW anyway. Keep the receipt: it names the exact bytes that were decided, so a finding can be reproduced instead of described.
      • A false DENY is not a security finding, but it is worth knowing, because a guard that refuses too much gets turned off by the people who have to live with it.
      • The known boundary, stated up front so nobody thinks they discovered it. Intent that lives inside the payload is not read. A write whose payload says DELETE FROM ... is a write, and it will be allowed if the write itself is well-formed and scoped. That is not a bug in this guard, it is the edge of what any check on shape and scope can do. It is the unsolved part of the problem, which is why this page exists rather than a claim that the problem is solved.
      • Matching is exact by design. A known action in a different case is treated as unrecognised and refused. Refusing a near-miss is the fail-closed choice.

      What this shows, and what it does not

      Two components, one page, one paper. The bench above demonstrates the shape-and-scope guard: it decides whether a proposal is well-formed and in scope, and it runs a demonstration policy that is deliberately not the production one. The paper describes a different component, the quorum gate, which decides whether enough distinct seats signed the same bytes. The four defect classes the paper reports cannot be reproduced here, because none of them live in this guard. What the two share is the discipline: every verdict carries the bytes it was taken over, so a refusal can be checked instead of believed.

      • It shows the default. A proposal the guard cannot positively recognise is refused, the refusal carries a reason code, and the decision carries a digest over the bytes. A conformant proposal passes, which matters: a guard that refuses everything proves nothing.
      • It is a demonstration policy, not the production one. The action vocabulary here is a demonstration set. The policy that governs the real runtime is not published, is not in this page, and cannot be read from it.
      • Any public gate can be queried. Someone determined could map whatever policy sits behind it, one proposal at a time. That is exactly why this one runs a demonstration policy rather than the real one.
      • It does not judge whether a proposal is wise. The guard checks shape and scope. A conformant, correctly scoped proposal that is strategically wrong still returns ALLOW. The checker that would judge intent does not exist yet.
      • Nothing here is the runtime. There is nothing behind this check, nothing to take, and no system it can touch. It is one guard.
